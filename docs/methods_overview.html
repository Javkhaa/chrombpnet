Methods & Algorithm Reference

Reading cell types out of cell-free DNA

00The idea, and the biology under it

Why a blood draw contains a record of which tissues are dying.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is DNA shed into blood by dying cells. When a cell dies, nucleases chew its genome down to the fragments that were wrapped around nucleosomes and thus protected — so a cfDNA fragment is a footprint of where a nucleosome sat in the cell of origin. Because nucleosome positioning differs by cell type, the pool of fragments carries a mixed signature of which cell types contributed. Deconvolution is the inverse problem: given the mixture, estimate the per-cell-type proportions.

We read that signature two independent ways. They rely on different physics, fail in different situations, and — when they agree — give real confidence.

# Signal 1 — where fragments are DEPLETED open chromatin (active promoters/enhancers) has no nucleosome to protect it → cfDNA coverage dips there → dip depth reports that cell type's accessibility # Signal 2 — the RHYTHM of fragments in gene bodies transcribed genes have disordered nucleosome arrays; silent genes keep regular ones → the ~190 bp periodicity of fragment protection reports transcription state

01The reference atlas

What we compare the cfDNA against.

Both methods score a sample against a reference of 346 single-cell ATAC cell types (adult subset = 244), each with a genome-wide chromatin accessibility track. ATAC marks open chromatin, which is a proxy for what a cell type is doing. The reference is the only place cell-type identity enters — the cfDNA feature is matched against each cell type's accessibility to ask "does this sample look like it contains that cell type?"

A known limitation, worth stating up front: ATAC only sees the open compartment (~0.1–11% of the genome carries signal). That is well-matched to Signal 1 (which lives at open regulatory elements) but is a proxy — not a direct nucleosome map — for Signal 2.

02Method 1 — Coverage-dip composition deconvolution

The workhorse. Turns fragment coverage into per-cell-type fractions. deconv_stream.py · griffin_grouped.py

The pipeline, end to end

Coverage dip → simplex solve

For each cell type we take its most cell-type-specific accessible sites (its markers), measure how deep the cfDNA coverage dips there, and solve for the mix of cell types whose combined marker-dips best reproduce what we observe.

What it measures

GC-corrected composite coverage dip of cfDNA fragments at each cell type's marker sites. Deep dip = that cell type's regulatory regions are open = it contributed.

How it solves

Simplex-constrained least squares y = R·w: weights w ≥ 0, sum to 1, plus an unknown component for cell types not in the atlas. No ridge (l2 = 0), validated by spike-in recovery.

What you get

Per-cell-type (or per-group) fractions, a rolled-up hematopoietic mass, and recon_r = how well the fit reproduces the observed dips.

When it works / fails

works whole-genome cfDNA at depth. fails shallow or targeted panels that don't cover the marker sites — the dip vanishes into noise.

Two design details matter. Similarity grouping (--merge-corr): many cell types have near-identical accessibility (all the T-cell subsets, say), which makes the math unstable. We agglomeratively merge cell types whose signatures correlate above a threshold, so the reference stays well-conditioned — fine where cell types are distinguishable, collapsed where they aren't. GC correction reweights fragments so sequencing GC-bias doesn't masquerade as biology.

03Method 2 — Block B: gene-body nucleosome phasing

The genome-wide, depth-robust alternative. genebody_wps.py

The pipeline, end to end

Windowed Protection Score → FFT → tissue-of-origin

Inside each gene body we measure the rhythm of nucleosome protection, extract how strong that ~190 bp rhythm is per gene, and correlate the whole profile against each cell type's expression state.

What it measures

The periodicity of the Windowed Protection Score (WPS) in gene bodies. A Fourier transform pulls out the power at the nucleosome-repeat length; the per-gene ratio is nratio.

How it calls cell type

Affinity = correlation of the sample's per-gene phasing against each cell type's accessibility (z-scored across cell types). Active genes phase weakly, so the contributing cell type correlates negatively.

What you get

A spectral peak (QC: should sit at ~190 bp), per-gene nratio, and a ranked cell-type / lineage affinity profile.

When it works / fails

works genome-wide, robust to low or uneven depth — it ran where Method 1 couldn't. weaker as a fraction estimator; it ranks, it doesn't cleanly quantify.

Why bother with a second method: it is genome-wide. Method 1 lives at the ~0.1–11% of the genome ATAC can see; Block B reads every gene body, so it survives targeted panels and shallow samples that starve the dip. On healthy plasma the two agree — both land on hematopoietic — which is the cross-check that says the signal is real, not an artifact of one feature.

04Glossary — every term in one place

The vocabulary that shows up in the logs and figures.

cfDNA
Cell-free DNA — fragments shed into blood by dying cells; a nucleosome footprint of the cell of origin.
coverage dip
Local drop in cfDNA fragment coverage over open chromatin (no nucleosome to protect the DNA). Depth of the dip encodes accessibility.The feature Method 1 deconvolves.
WPS
Windowed Protection Score — at each base, (fragments fully spanning a 120 bp window) minus (fragments with an end inside it). High where a nucleosome sits, low in linkers.
nucleosome repeat (~190 bp)
The spacing of the nucleosome array. Its signature appears as a spectral peak at ~185–200 bp in the WPS Fourier transform.If this peak is absent, the sample has no usable phasing signal.
nratio
Per gene, nucleosome-band FFT power (period 150–250 bp) ÷ broadband power. A depth-robust index of how strongly that gene's nucleosomes are phased.
affinity
Correlation of a sample's per-gene phasing against a cell type's z-scored accessibility. Most-negative = most source-like.How Block B ranks cell types.
z-scoring
Standardizing each gene's accessibility across cell types, so the shared "housekeeping" signal drops out and cell-type-specific differences drive the ranking.
merge-corr
Threshold for merging cell types with near-identical accessibility into one group, keeping the reference matrix well-conditioned.
simplex / unknown
The solver constrains weights to be non-negative and sum to 1 (a simplex). An extra unknown column absorbs cell types absent from the atlas so their mass doesn't distort the rest.
recon_r
Correlation between observed and reconstructed feature — the fit quality. Below ~0.6 the call is unreliable and is excluded.
hematopoietic mass
Summed weight of all blood/immune cell types. Healthy plasma is ~90%+ — the expected ground truth.
GC correction
Reweighting fragments by GC content so library/sequencing GC-bias isn't mistaken for a coverage difference.
trim compensation
--trim N extends both fragment ends by N bp to undo upstream hard-trimming (e.g. bisulfite M-bias trimming), so fragment lengths are correct before filtering.
permutation null
Re-running a classifier on shuffled labels many times to see what AUC chance produces. An AUC is only real if it clears this null.

The cross-check that matters

On healthy plasma, the coverage-dip method and the nucleosome-phasing method — which share no features and fail for different reasons — both land on hematopoietic. That agreement is the strongest evidence the composition read-out is real.

Honest scope

These methods resolve the dominant composition of a cfDNA sample (what is it mostly made of — reliably, blood for healthy plasma). They do not detect a small disease-specific fraction — circulating tumor DNA in cancer, or brain-derived cfDNA in ALS — which sits below what a coarse nucleosome/accessibility read-out can see. For those, the matched signal is methylation (present in the WGBS data, not yet used), which carries dense, cell-type-specific marks per fragment.
